# What Should an Accounting Practice Automate First? 12 High-Value Opportunities

The first automation matters because it sets the team's expectations for everything that follows. Choose a vague or overly complex process and staff may conclude that automation creates work. Choose a frequent, rules-based process with a visible result and the practice builds confidence quickly.

The priority should be the workflow that creates the best combination of value, simplicity and control.

## 1. Website enquiry capture

Record every genuine website enquiry in a defined system and preserve the source.

## 2. Enquiry acknowledgement

Send a useful confirmation so the prospect knows the request has been received and understands the next step.

## 3. Lead qualification

Collect basic approved information before assigning the enquiry to the right person.

## 4. Appointment booking

Give suitable prospects a clear path to book and automate confirmations and reminders.

## 5. Missed appointment follow-up

Create a respectful follow-up path when a prospect or client misses an appointment.

## 6. Client onboarding

Coordinate forms, documents, meetings and internal handovers through a repeatable workflow.

## 7. Identity and information requests

Track required items and make outstanding requirements visible.

## 8. Document reminders

Send controlled reminders when requested information remains outstanding and stop when the requirement is satisfied.

## 9. Recurring workflow creation

Create standard task sequences for repeatable services with owners, due dates and checkpoints.

## 10. Internal notifications

Alert the right team member when a workflow reaches a stage requiring human action.

## 11. Client status updates

Where appropriate, trigger factual communications from real workflow events.

## 12. Reviews and re-engagement

After suitable completed work, request feedback. With appropriate permissions, build useful re-engagement processes for dormant relationships.

## How to prioritise the list

Score each opportunity from one to five for frequency, staff time, error risk and ease of standardisation. Add the scores.

High-scoring processes deserve attention first, but only if the practice can define clear controls and ownership.

## What should remain human?

Automation should support the practice, not blur professional responsibility. Advice, judgement, unusual circumstances and sensitive client decisions require appropriate human oversight.

## Use a value-risk matrix, not enthusiasm

Score each automation candidate against five factors: frequency, staff time, client impact, rule clarity and risk. High-frequency, high-effort processes with clear rules usually belong near the top. High-risk processes that depend heavily on judgement should move down the list or retain strong human approval.

A CFO can add a sixth factor: economic value. Estimate annual staff hours released and any measurable effect on revenue conversion, WIP or debtor follow-up. Compare that with implementation and support cost.

The practice should also consider opportunity cost. Automating a process that saves 30 hours a month may be more valuable than improving a low-volume workflow that partners happen to dislike.

Security and data quality can change the priority. If a workflow depends on poor-quality data or weak account controls, fix the foundation first. Likewise, if management cannot currently measure the process, establish the reporting baseline before promising a return.

This prevents the common mistake of automating whatever looks easiest in a demo. The right first project is the one that creates a credible commercial result with acceptable risk.

## Sequence the roadmap around constraints

The highest-scoring automation is not always the first one to deploy. A practice may have a busy compliance period, a system migration or a security issue that changes the timing. Prioritisation should therefore include readiness as well as value.

If a workflow depends on clean client data, resolve the data problem first. If it depends on an integration that is still being scoped, choose another process for the pilot. A good roadmap balances expected return with implementation capacity so the firm does not create change fatigue while trying to save time.

## Questions to take into the partner or management meeting

Before committing to the change, management should be able to answer five practical questions. What problem are we solving? What does the process cost us today? Which result will show that the change worked? Which risks or approvals must remain under human control? Who owns the process after go-live?

Those questions keep the technology conversation grounded in capacity, cash, client service and control. They also make it easier to compare providers because the practice is evaluating an operating outcome rather than a feature list.

## Frequently asked questions

### What accounting task should be automated first?

Choose a high-frequency administrative task with clear rules and an easy way to measure improvement.

### Is document chasing suitable for automation?

It can be, provided requests, reminder timing, stop conditions and escalation rules are clearly defined.

### Can client communication be automated?

Routine, approved and event-based messages can be automated, but sensitive or advice-related communication should remain human.

### Is more automation always better?

No. The right amount of automation is the amount that improves the process without reducing trust, control or quality.

## Pick one and prove it

Do not launch twelve workflows at once. Choose one, establish a baseline, implement carefully and prove the result before expanding.
