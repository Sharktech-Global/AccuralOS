# AI for Accountants in Sydney: How Practices Can Automate Client Work

For a Sydney accounting practice, growth can create an uncomfortable trade-off. More clients bring more opportunity, but they also create more coordination, more follow-up and more pressure on partners and managers.

AI and workflow automation are useful when they remove that friction. The commercial goal is to protect senior capacity, improve the client experience and make work easier to see before deadlines become urgent.

## Where Sydney accounting practices can use automation

Start with processes that happen every week: enquiries, bookings, onboarding, document collection, recurring client work and internal handovers.

A practical workflow might capture a new website enquiry, acknowledge it, collect basic information, offer a consultation and notify the correct team member. Another workflow might request onboarding documents, track outstanding items and alert staff when a client needs personal assistance.

## Reduce enquiry delays

Prospects may contact several firms. A defined response process helps ensure an enquiry is recorded and acknowledged even when staff are in meetings or working on client deadlines.

Automation should not pretend to provide professional advice. Its job is to move the prospect to the right human conversation.

## Improve document follow-up

Document chasing can consume time across tax, BAS, bookkeeping and advisory work. Standard request templates, tracked outstanding items and controlled reminders can reduce reliance on staff memory.

When a request becomes unusual or sensitive, the workflow should escalate it to a person.

## Create clearer practice visibility

Partners and practice managers need to know which jobs are moving, which are blocked and which clients need attention. A shared operational view can reduce internal status chasing and make exceptions easier to identify.

## A Sydney practice automation plan

1. Select one high-volume workflow.
2. Record every manual step.
3. Identify the information required.
4. Define staff approval points.
5. Configure the workflow.
6. Test common and unusual scenarios.
7. Train the team.
8. Measure response time, completion time and manual effort.
9. Improve the workflow.
10. Expand only after the first process is stable.

## What a Sydney practice should measure

A partner or CFO should judge automation by the operating measures that matter to the firm. Start with enquiry response time, onboarding cycle time, outstanding client information, WIP ageing, partner review bottlenecks and non-billable administrative hours.

For a growing practice, one particularly useful question is whether revenue can increase faster than support headcount. Automation will not remove the need for capable people, but it can stop growth from automatically creating the same percentage increase in coordination work.

The control side matters as well. Sydney firms handling sensitive client information should review Microsoft 365 access, MFA, email authentication, backups and staff security awareness as workflows become more connected. A Security Health Check can provide a baseline. While Power BI can give directors a clearer view of WIP, lock-up, client mix and other agreed practice KPIs.

## A sensible 90-day test

Do not judge the rollout on launch day. Give the first workflow enough time to run through real client cases, then compare it with the baseline. A Sydney practice should be able to show whether response time improved. Whether fewer onboarding items stalled and whether partners spent less time intervening in routine administration.

If the measures improve but staff dislike the process, investigate adoption and usability before expanding. If staff like the system but the numbers do not move, revisit the workflow design. The next automation should only be approved when the first one has produced a result the practice can explain in commercial terms.

## Questions to take into the partner or management meeting

Before committing to the change, management should be able to answer five practical questions. What problem are we solving? What does the process cost us today? Which result will show that the change worked? Which risks or approvals must remain under human control? Who owns the process after go-live?
