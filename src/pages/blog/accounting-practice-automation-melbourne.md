# Accounting Practice Automation in Melbourne: Where AI Can Remove Repetitive Admin

Accounting practice automation should improve consistency without making the client experience feel mechanical. For Melbourne firms with multiple service lines or growing teams, the strongest opportunity is often to standardise the administrative layer around professional work.

That means clearer enquiry handling, repeatable onboarding, controlled follow-up and better visibility over where work is waiting.

## Start with repetitive administration

List the tasks that occur across many clients. Enquiry handling, appointment reminders, onboarding, document collection, recurring job setup and internal notifications are good examples.

The aim is not to automate judgement. It is to reduce the manual coordination surrounding professional work.

## Build a better enquiry process

A connected enquiry workflow can record the lead, acknowledge the request, collect approved information and offer a booking path. The accountant then receives a more organised handover.

## Standardise onboarding

A practice can define the normal onboarding journey once. Forms, document requests, meetings and internal tasks can be tracked against that journey. Exceptions can be escalated instead of every client requiring manual coordination from scratch.

## Reduce document chasing

Controlled reminders can help practices follow up missing information without relying on a staff member's memory. The workflow should stop when information is received and move unusual matters to a person.

## Make workloads visible

Practice managers need visibility across jobs, owners, deadlines and blockers. A shared view can help teams identify work that needs attention before it becomes urgent.

## A practical Melbourne automation roadmap

Begin with one workflow and establish a baseline. Measure manual touches, delays and staff time. Configure the process with clear human approval points. Test it with real scenarios. Train staff and review results after launch.

Only then move to the next process.

## The finance view: protect margin and reduce avoidable variation

In a professional-services business, small process differences can compound into margin leakage. Two teams may perform the same onboarding differently, chase documents at different times and record status in different places. The result is more rework, more partner intervention and less reliable management information.

A CFO or practice leader should therefore track the effect of automation on:

- non-billable administration
- write-offs and rework
- turnaround time
- WIP ageing and lock-up
- staff capacity by service line
- client response and completion rates

Automation is most defensible when it standardises the routine path and makes exceptions visible. The exception is where a skilled person adds value.

For Melbourne firms, local implementation support can also matter during rollout. Sharktech has a Melbourne office in South Yarra. And the wider service capability can include cybersecurity reviews and Power BI reporting where the practice needs stronger control or better management visibility.

The objective is not to turn accounting into a factory. It is to remove avoidable process variation so professional time is spent where judgement matters.

## What success should look like after one quarter

After a full quarter, management should be able to point to fewer manual follow-ups, more consistent onboarding and better visibility over work that is waiting. The important question is whether those changes are visible in margin, WIP or team capacity, not just in user activity inside the platform.

Where the expected result has not appeared, review the process before buying more technology. The issue may be poor adoption, unclear ownership, bad source data or a workflow that automated the wrong step. That review discipline is what turns an automation project into an operating improvement program.

## Questions to take into the partner or management meeting

Before committing to the change, management should be able to answer five practical questions. What problem are we solving? What does the process cost us today? Which result will show that the change worked? Which risks or approvals must remain under human control? Who owns the process after go-live?

Those questions keep the technology conversation grounded in capacity, cash, client service and control. They also make it easier to compare providers because the practice is evaluating an operating outcome rather than a feature list.
